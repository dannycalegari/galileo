# Duke Humfrey's Library, upstairs in the Bodleian (the map's library=): Rouse turns back anyone who
# is not a reader while he is in the building, and it is locked while he is away (main.cc, mayEnter).
# You become a reader by bringing him a letter from a Doctor of the University (Bainbridge's, for the
# Dialogue or a spyglass) and swearing the readers' oath.
guarded Duke Humfrey's Library
  by rouse
  pass if player reader-bodleian
  stop (John Rouse is at the foot of the stairs before you are.) Not up there, sir, if you please. The Library is for graduates of the University, and for strangers who bring a letter from one of its Doctors and swear the readers' oath. I make no exceptions; I have made none for an Earl. {player set heard-reader-rule}
  shut The door at the foot of the library stairs is locked. Sir Thomas Bodley allowed no candle or fire among his books, so the Library is open only by daylight, from eight till eleven and from two till five, and Master Rouse keeps the key.
end

    library,bodley,bodleian,rouse,letter if player has:bainbridge-letter: Give it to Rouse, at the Schools. He will make you swear an oath. Swear it.
    harriot,savile,sunspots,spots,papers,secret if player read-harriot: You found Harriot's papers? Sir Henry showed them to me when I came up in 1619, and made me promise. Harriot saw the mountains of the Moon before anyone, and the spots on the Sun, and Jupiter's moons a little after Galileo, and printed nothing at all. He has been dead eleven years; I suppose the promise died with him. But I should not like to be the one who tells Florence.
    job: I teach the astronomy of Ptolemy, of Copernicus and of Tycho, all three, as Sir Henry Savile's statutes require, and let the young men choose. I wrote a book on the comet of 1618, and I am making tables of the stars.
    spyglass,telescope,glass if player has:spyglass: A perspective glass! May I? (He takes it to the window and stays there a long while.) Galileo was right about the Moon. I shall say so in my lectures, and the Proctors may say what they like.
    galileo,copernicus,dialogue: Galileo's moons of Jupiter I have seen myself, through a poor glass; I could wish for a better. As for the Earth moving, I teach it as a hypothesis. A very good hypothesis.
  end
end

person wilkins
  name John Wilkins
  title a student of Magdalen Hall
  role student
  near Magdalen
  talk
    name: John Wilkins, of Magdalen Hall. My grandfather was John Dod, the preacher; I am not a preacher yet. {name}
    job: I am reading for my degree, and in my own time I am reading Galileo and Kepler, which is more useful. I think the Moon is a world like ours, with seas and mountains and perhaps people.
    harriot,moon,map,sunspots,spots if player read-harriot: An Englishman drew the Moon through a glass before Galileo did? In 1609? Then I shall say so in my book, when I write it, and the Italians may be as cross as they like.
    moon,world,flying,fly if player saw-moons: You have seen the moons of Jupiter with your own eyes? Then you know: other worlds, with their own moons. One day we shall fly to ours. Not by witchcraft: by an engine, with wings, or by a chariot, if one could get above the pull of the Earth.
    moon,world,flying,fly: If the Moon is a world, then why should not a man go there? Birds fly; why not a chariot, with wings and springs? Do not laugh. Everyone laughs. I am going to write a book about it.
  end
end
